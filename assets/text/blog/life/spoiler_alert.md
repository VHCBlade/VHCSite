I was playing board games at home with my friends, and after some dinner and games, it was finally time to leave. As they were preparing to leave, I suddenly remembered that I had recently watched K-Pop Demon Hunters. So, as I normally do, I wanted to discuss it with them.

It had been 3 months since the release of the movie, and I wanted to discuss the cultural impact that the movie has had. The strengths and weaknesses the movie had, as well as what its success says about the wider culture.

To my surprise, one of my friends asked to not talk about it since he had been avoiding spoilers since it came out. He had been procrastinating watching it, but had been diligent about avoiding spoilers.

All of a sudden, the conversation was completely side tracked, and I was reminded of one of my longstanding convictions.

I hate Anti-Spoiler culture.

-------------------

# The Deification of Personal Novelty
-------------------

The first issue with avoiding spoilers is that it places an undue value on one’s own novel experience.

The adherents to this Anti-Spoiler Culture will take online fasts to avoid discussion about movies.

They’ll walk out of conversations with friends and family, in a bid to maintain their spoiler purity.

In some extreme cases, they'll even police strangers and implore them to adhere to this spoiler censorship.

They place a value on avoiding spoilers that I would argue should only be reserved for God Himself. (Though writing this has made me realize that I should have more fervor for God)

If you prefer to avoid spoilers, you shouldn’t deify the pursuit. Instead, you should treat it as a preference.

If a friend serves fries when you prefer onion rings, would you accuse him of sabotage?

If you love vanilla ice cream, but the buffet only has cookies and cream, would you throw a fit?

Avoiding spoilers should be a nice-to-have but if you don't get it then it's fine. The spoiler culture that we have found ourselves in has come to treat being “spoiled” as an unforgivable sin. Is this how you would treat your friend serving you food you dislike?

Moreover, if not being spoiled is truly your preference, then you should actively seek to be spoiled sometimes. To remind yourself that it isn't the greatest good, or even close to it. 

Place this love for personal novelty in its proper place, as a preference, and not the purpose of your life.

# The Denial of Communion
-------------------

The second issue with avoiding spoilers is that it denies communion with your conversation partners.

The purpose of culture is to create a shared reality that its recipients can live in. It creates something common between people and reinforces their shared identity. To deny this communion for the sake of preserving your own novelty is a deeply unnatural end. It is antithetical to the very purpose of culture. Rather than to be a tool to further the bonds between friends, it becomes a barrier that limits their shared life.

Moreover, the spoilers can become a stumbling block that disrupts the harmony between friends. Accusations of betrayal or meanness can be flung with little regard if this unspoken spoiler agreement is broken. Simply sharing your excitement for a movie can suddenly become an argument about being inconsiderate.

Instead, you should evaluate the true value of avoiding spoilers. Is it really comparable to the value of entering into a shared life with your friends or strangers? What is it that people regret in their lives?

Do they regret knowing that Darth Vader was related to Luke before watching Star Wars?

Of course not! Instead they wish that they had made more friends or spent more time with their family.

Set aside your obsession with avoiding spoilers and embrace the communion you can have with your friends.

# The Cult of the New
-------------------

The last issue with avoiding spoilers is that it reveals an undue fascination with the new. The new is seen as exciting and good, and by comparison, the old is seen as dull and uninteresting.

It’s why people will pay full price to play a new game rather than wait for a sale. The new and shiny is alluring and is seen as intrinsically valuable.

Furthermore, people often think that they'll rise to the occasion when some disaster happens, as if the added stress actually makes them better. But is that really the case? Will the novelty really bring out the best in us?

Rather than novelty, virtue is found in everyday living. It's found in the steady improvement of your disposition towards the mundane. You don’t become brave all of a sudden when a crisis comes, instead your everyday practice of bravery equips you to become the hero you need to be when the time comes.

Moreover, caring about spoilers misplaces the proper place of novelty in our lives. Instead of coming from taking risks in our lives, we find it from watching movies or reading stories. The stories, rather than inspire us to be heroes on the adventure of our life, become a misplaced source of novelty.

Find novelty in the adventure of your life, and appreciate the stories as you live your mundane life.

-------------------

# The Proper Place
-------------------

While avoiding spoilers can certainly be a valid preference, it shouldn't be anywhere near the center of your life. And the anti-spoiler culture that we find ourselves in places it as one of its highest values.

In fact, some stories, such as mysteries and thrillers, are probably better unspoiled. However, even with these, the value of an unspoiled experience must still be placed under the value of communion with other people.

After all, the greatest story of all, has already been spoiled. We know that in the end, God wins. And that knowledge gives us hope as we go about the story of our lives.

All things must be placed in their proper place. So learn to appreciate a story even when it's spoiled.
